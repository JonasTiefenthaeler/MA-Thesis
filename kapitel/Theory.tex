\chapter{Theory}\label{chap: Theory}

Motions of viscous fluids are described by a set of equations, called the \acrfull{nse} ZITAT.
To simulate the behaviour of fluid motion theses partial differential equations have to be integrated by the simulation code.
They consist of the mass conservation equation, also called the continuity equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq: conservation equation}
    \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i} = 0
\end{equation}
and the momentum equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq: momentum equation}
    \rho \left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t} + u_j \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\right) = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i} + \mu\frac{\partial^2 u_i}{\partial x_j \partial x_j} + \rho g_i
\end{equation}
both shown for the incompressible regime, which is considered throughout this work (\cite{NekoGoverningEquations}).
Additionally, the Einstein summation convention is used in this notation and due to the incompressible regime, $\rho$ and $\mu$ are constants.\\
The flow behaviour of viscous fluids can be separated into two regimes.
Laminar flow, turbulent flow and the transition between them.
In laminar flow, the fluid particles move in parallel paths and are constant in time. 
In turbulent flow, the instantaneous movement of the fluid is stochastic, while the mean flow is dependent by the flow condition and the shape of the flow domain.
Only fully turbulent flows are considered in this work.\\
In the following chapter, first, the basics of wall bounded turbulence behaviour is explained, followed by an explanation of the surface roughness.


\section{Description of turbulent wall bounded flows}\label{sec: Description of turbulent flows}

To be able to understand turbulent behaviour in a rough regime, it is first important, to give an overview of turbulent wall bounded flows in general, since it gives the bases for the interpretations later in this thesis VERWEIS AUF KAPITEL.

\paragraph{Wall shear stress}\label{par: wall shear stress}


\begin{equation}
    \tau_w = \mu\frac{dU}{dy}
\end{equation}
and is solely based on viscosity (\cite{popeTurbulentFlows2015}).
The viscosity is dominant at the wall, hence the wall shear stress is the only stress active at the wall and it keeps being dominant in the near wall region.
With a growing distance to the wall the influence of viscosity decreases, while the importance of the Reynolds stresses, described in later in this section, increases.


\paragraph{Friction velocity}\label{par: Friction velocity}

Using the wall shear stress and the density $\rho$ the friction velocity is defined as
\begin{equation}
    u_\tau = \sqrt{\frac{\tau_w}{\rho}}
\end{equation}
The friction velocity $u_\tau$ is a viscous scale and is used to propery scale velocity values in the near-wall region.

\paragraph{Viscous length scale}\label{par: viscous length scale}
The viscous length scale
\begin{equation}\label{eq: delta nu}
    \delta_{\nu} = \frac{\nu}{u_{\tau}}
\end{equation}
is similar to the friction velocity $u_\tau$ a viscous scale and is used to scale the length scale in the near-wall region.
\paragraph{Friction reynolds number}
The friction Reynolds number is defined as
\begin{equation} \label{eq: ReTau}
    Re_{\tau} = \frac{u_{\tau} \cdot \delta}{\nu} = \frac{\delta}{\delta_{\nu}}
\end{equation}
which states directly, that $Re_{\tau}$ describes the ratio between the largest and smallest turbulent scales.
\paragraph{Stress tensor}\label{par: RStress tensor}
The stress tensor of a turbulent flow is a second order symmetric tensor and its components are the Reynolds stresses, which leads to 
\begin{equation} \label{eq: Reynolds stresses}
    R_{ij} = \langle u'_i u'_j \rangle
\end{equation}
The diagonal components $\langle u'_iu'_i\rangle$ are the normal stresses and the off diagonal components $\langle u'_1 u'_2 \rangle$ and $\langle u'_2u'_1 \rangle$ denote the shear stresses.
It is important to note that $\langle u'_3 \rangle = 0$, as described in \cite{popeTurbulentFlows2015}.

\subsection{Plus units}\label{subsec: plus units}
Using the viscous scale $\delta_{\nu}$ and $u_{\tau}$ the viscous units or wall units $U^+$ and $y^+$ can be derived using the equations
\begin{equation}\label{eq: U plus}
    U^+ = \frac{U}{u_{\tau}}
\end{equation}
and 
\begin{equation}\label{eq: y plus}
    y^+ = \frac{y}{\delta_{\nu}}
\end{equation}
with the superscription of + indicating the scaling in wall units.
Using the scaling of the wall units, velocity profiles of turbulent wall bounded flows collapse onto another, independent of the Reynolds number.
Also, the velocity profiles can be divided into three main regions.
The viscous sublayer where $y^+ < 5$, the logarithmic region where $y^+ > 30$ and the buffer region in between.
Within the viscous sublayer, the law of the wall $U^+ = y^+$ holds. 
Within the logarithmic region, the log law $U^+ = \frac{1}{\kappa}\ln{y^+} + A$, with $\kappa$ and $A$ can be chosen according to the individual case. 
In this thesis $\kappa = 0.41$ and $A = 5.2$ are used, according to \cite{popeTurbulentFlows2015}.

\section{Roughness}\label{sec: Roughness}

\subsection{Roughness parameters}\label{subsec: Roughness parameters}

To be able to describe rough surfaces, a multitude of roughness parameters exist, as described by \cite{chung_predicting_2021}.
The considered roughness parameters in this study are:

\paragraph{Average roughness height}

The average roughness height is the mean deviation  of the surface from the virtual origin $y=0$
\begin{equation}\label{eq: avg roughness height}
    S_a = \sum_{i,j}^{M,N} |h_{i,j}| / (MN)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Root-mean-squeare roughness height}

\begin{equation}
    S_q = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j}^{M,N} h_{i,j}^{2}} / (MN)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Skewness}
The skewness is a measure of asymmetry of a rough surface \cite{chung_predicting_2021}.
Surface elevations lead to a positive skewness, while surface retractions result in a negatively skewed surface.
\begin{equation}
    Sk = \sum_{i,j}^{M,N} h^{3}_{i,j} /(MNS_{q}^{3})
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Maximum peak-to-valley height}
The maximum peak-to-valley height is the difference between the highest and lowest point of the rough surface
\begin{equation}
    S_{Z_{max}} = max(h_{i,j}) - min(h_{i,j})
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Effective slope}
The effective slope is the absolute gradient of the rough surface as described by \cite{napoli_effect_2008}.
\begin{equation}
    ES = \frac{1}{L_x L_y} \int_0^{L_x} \int_0^{L_y} \left| \frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}x} \right|_{i,j} \mathrm{d}x \, \mathrm{d}y
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Mean peak-to-valley height}
The calculation of the mean peak to valley height $S_m$ is done according to \cite{thakkar_investigation_2017}.
The method is dividing the surface into tiles and calculating the difference between the highest and lowest point for each tile.
The mean of the differences results in  the mean peak-to-valley height $k$, which is also used as the measurement for roughness height in this thesis, according to \cite{de_maio_direct_2023}.
For this study the rough surface is divided into 25 rectangular tiles of equal size.
Equal size means in this context, that the same number of grid points are used for the length and width of the tiles.


\subsection{Virtual origin}\label{subsec: Virtual origin}

The virtual origin is a reference plane, which is taken as the basis for measuring the wall distance $y$ (\cite{chung_predicting_2021}).
In a smooth wall case, there is no virtual origin, since all data can be calculated from the wall itself, hence the point where the no-slip condition is active (\cite{chan_systematic_2015}), which leads to consistent wall distances for every point in the field.\\
Yet, in a rough wall case, this is not as straightforward.
Due to wall roughness, the distance from e.g. the pipe center to the wall varies continuously over the whole flow field.
To be able to compute consistent data, a virtual origin has to be defined. 
There are many different approaches to defining the virtual origin.
Notable methods are the usage of the mean momentum absorption plane by \cite{thom_momentum_1971} or the determination of the position of the mean streamwise velocity being zero, since it is assumed, that this is the equivalent position of the no slip condition in a smooth wall case.
A third approach is to take the mean radius of the rough surface for the virtual origin.\\
In the investigation by \cite{chan_systematic_2015}, all three of the approaches are compared. 
The approach of using the mean radius for the virtual origin seems to offer the best correlation between the considered smooth and rough cases in this study.

\subsection{Influence of surface roughness on drag}\label{subsec: Roughness influence on drag}

In turbulen flows over rough surfaces, the log law for the velocity profile described in \ref{subsec: plus units}, experiences a downshift AUF PLOT VERWEISEN.
This downshift is the difference between the velocity profiles of turbulent flows with a smooth and a rough wall, at the same friction reynolds number $Re_{\tau}$ and can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \Delta U^+(k_s^+) = \frac{1}{\kappa}\ln{k_s^+} + A - B_s(k_s^+) 
\end{equation}
with $k_s^+$ representing the equivalent sand-grain roughness.
It is the roughness height $k_s$ scaled in wall units bs $k_s^+ = \frac{k_s}{\delta_{\nu}}$.
The definition of $k_s$ does not follow a universal law and its choice varies for different surfaces, but there are studies, that try to link certain surface topographies to $k_s$, like \cite{flack_review_2010}.
Additionally, the research of \cite{flack_skin_2020} indicates, that a measure of height of the roughness elements ($S_q$ or $S_m$), the Skewness $Sq$ and the effective slope $ES$ are the most influential parameters to $k_s$.
