Motions of viscous fluids are described by a set of equations, called the \acrfull{nse} ZITAT.
To simulate the behaviour of fluid motion theses partial differential equations have to be integrated by the simulation code.
They consist of the mass conservation equation, also called the continuity equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq: conservation equation}
    \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i} = 0
\end{equation}
and the momentum equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq: momentum equation}
    \rho \left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t} + u_j \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\right) = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i} + \mu\frac{\partial^2 u_i}{\partial x_j \partial x_j} + \rho g_i
\end{equation}
both shown for the incompressible regime, which is considered throughout this work (\cite{NekoGoverningEquations}).
Additionally, the Einstein summation convention is used in this notation and due to the incompressible regime, $\rho$ and $\mu$ are constants.\\
The flow behaviour of viscous fluids can be separated into two regimes.
Laminar flow, turbulent flow and the transition between them.
In laminar flow, the fluid particles move in parallel paths and are constant in time. 
In turbulent flow, the instantaneous movement of the fluid is stochastic, while the mean flow is dependent by the flow condition and the shape of the flow domain.
Only fully turbulent flows are considered in this work.\\
In the following chapter, first, the basics of wall bounded turbulence behaviour is explained, followed by an explanation of the surface roughness.

The viscous units are introduced, which are used to nondimensionalize the emerging quantities in turbulent flows.
The two scaling quantities are
\begin{equation}\label{eq: viscous scaling units}
    \text{Friction velocity: }\quad u_\tau = \sqrt{\frac{\tau_w}{\rho}} \qquad \text{Viscous length scale:}\quad \delta_\nu = \frac{\nu}{u_\tau}
\end{equation}
while $u_\tau$ is used to scale velocity scales and $\delta_\nu$ scales length scales.
They are both dependent on the fluid properties $\rho$ and $\nu$, as well as the wall shear stress $\tau_w$ which is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq: wall shear stress}
   \text{Wall shear stress: } \quad \tau_w = \mu \frac{d U}{dy}
\end{equation}
and quantifies the friction of the flow at the wall due to the viscosity of the fluid.
The viscosity is dominant at the wall, hence the wall shear stress is the only stress active at the wall and it keeps being dominant in the near wall region.
With a growing distance to the wall the influence of viscosity decreases, while the importance of the Reynolds stresses, described in later in this section, increases (\cite{popeTurbulentFlows2015}).\\
Using the friction velocity $u_\tau$, the viscosity $\nu$ and the characteristic length $\delta$, which is typically the radius R in a pipe, the friction Reynolds number $Re_\tau$ is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq: friction reynolds number}
   \text{Friction Reynolds number: }\quad Re_\tau = \frac{u_\tau \delta}{\nu} = \frac{\delta}{\delta_\nu}
\end{equation}
which is the non-dimensionalized Reynolds number in viscous scales.

Using the viscous scales $\delta_\nu$ and $u_\tau$, the non dimensionalized plus units $U^+$ and $y^+$ can be computed.
They are denoted by the $+$ superscript and are also called `viscous units' or `inner units' depending on the literature.
\begin{equation}
    U^+ = \frac{U}{u_\tau} \qquad \text{and} \qquad y^+ = \frac{y}{\delta_\nu}
\end{equation}

The respective lift above the log law line is expected for pipes, and reduces for channel flows (\cite{khouryTurbulentPipeFlow2014}).

The viscous sublayer where $y^+ < 5$, the logarithmic region where $y^+ > 30$ and the buffer region in between.
Within the viscous sublayer, the law of the wall $U^+ = y^+$ holds.

\paragraph{Stresses in turbulent flows}\label{par: stresses}
The stress tensor of a turbulent flow is a second order symmetric tensor and its components are the Reynolds stresses.
The stress tensor is denoted as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq: Reynolds stresses}
    R_{ij} = \langle u'_i u'_j \rangle
\end{equation}
The diagonal components $\langle u'_iu'_i\rangle$ are the normal stresses and the off diagonal components $\langle u'_1 u'_2 \rangle$ and $\langle u'_2u'_1 \rangle$ denote the shear stresses (\cite{popeTurbulentFlows2015}).
The prime supersrcipt ' denotes the instantaneous fluctuations, which are the instantaneous values subatracted from their respective mean.
Symbols with $\langle \rangle$ are averaged quantities.

\section{Roughness}\label{sec: Roughness}

\subsection{Roughness parameters}\label{subsec: Roughness parameters}

To be able to describe rough surfaces, a multitude of roughness parameters exist, as described by~\cite{chungPredictingDragRough2021}.
The influence of each parameter remains an open research question, which is focused on by many researches (\cite{flackReviewHydraulicRoughness2010},~\cite{jellyReynoldsDispersiveShear2018}).
However, it remains unclear, whether it is possible to associate individual surface parameters to specific flow characteristics, or if there is always a more general influence of the surface on the turbulent flow (\cite{chungPredictingDragRough2021}).\\
In order to be able to address this question and to keep the provided data comparable with current literature, it is important to consider a wide range of surface parameters. 
The considered roughness parameters in this work are:

\paragraph{Average roughness height}

The average roughness height is the mean deviation  of the surface from the virtual origin $y=0$
\begin{equation}\label{eq: avg roughness height}
    S_a = \sum_{i,j}^{M,N} |h_{i,j}| / (MN)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Root-mean-squeare roughness height}

\begin{equation}
    S_q = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j}^{M,N} h_{i,j}^{2}} / (MN)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Skewness}
The skewness is a measure of asymmetry of a rough surface~\cite{chungPredictingDragRough2021}.
Surface elevations lead to a positive skewness, while surface retractions result in a negatively skewed surface.
\begin{equation}
    Sk = \sum_{i,j}^{M,N} h^{3}_{i,j} /(MNS_{q}^{3})
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Maximum peak-to-valley height}
The maximum peak-to-valley height is the difference between the highest and lowest point of the rough surface
\begin{equation}
    S_{Z_{max}} = max(h_{i,j}) - min(h_{i,j})
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Effective slope}
The effective slope is the absolute gradient of the rough surface as described by~\cite{napoliEffectSlopeIrregularly2008}.
\begin{equation}
    ES = \frac{1}{L_x L_y} \int_0^{L_x} \int_0^{L_y} \left| \frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}x} \right|_{i,j} \mathrm{d}x \, \mathrm{d}y
\end{equation}

The method is dividing the surface into tiles and calculating the difference between the highest and lowest point for each tile.

It is important to note, that not all surface parameters are fully independent of each other.
Especially the height parameters, $S_a, S_q$ and $S_{z,5x5}$ have a dependence on each other, and cannot be chosen arbirarily for nonperiodic inhomogeneous roughness ZITAT.
The skewness and effective slope however can be controlled independently by the algorithm presented in \autoref{subsec: Surface generation algorithm}.

\subsection{Virtual origin}\label{subsec: Virtual origin}

The virtual origin is a reference plane, which is taken as the basis for measuring the wall distance $y$~(\cite{chungPredictingDragRough2021}).
In a smooth wall case, there is no virtual origin, since all data can be calculated from the wall itself, hence the point where the no-slip condition is active~(\cite{chanSystematicInvestigationRoughness2015}), which leads to consistent wall distances for every point in the field.\\
Yet, in a rough wall case, this is not as straightforward.
Due to wall roughness, the distance from e.g. the pipe center to the wall varies continuously over the whole flow field.
To be able to compute consistent data, a virtual origin, hence the position at which $y^+ = 0$ applies, has to be defined. 
There are many different approaches to defining the virtual origin.
Notable methods are the usage of the mean momentum absorption plane by~\cite{thomMomentumAbsorptionVegetation1971} or the determination of the position of the mean streamwise velocity being zero, since it is assumed, that this is the equivalent position of the no slip condition in a smooth wall case.
A third approach is to take the mean radius of the rough surface for the virtual origin.\\
In the investigation by~\cite{chanSystematicInvestigationRoughness2015}, all three of the approaches are compared. 
The approach of using the mean radius for the virtual origin seems to offer the best correlation between the considered smooth and rough cases in that particular study.
All data presented in this work define $y^+ = 0$ to be at the mean radius of the pipe.
